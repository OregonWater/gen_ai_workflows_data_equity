## The developers' question and the possibility of transfer

- What was happening in TAPs development when the question about what was ready to “dog-food” arose, and what did you understand that question to mean?
- What did it prompt you to reconsider about the GenAI-supported workflow?
- What seemed possible to package, demonstrate, or use outside TAPs? What still depended on the project's particular context?
- How did your understanding of the workflow change as you tried to describe what could travel to another setting?

When the dog-fooding question arose, I had just made a presentation for the Asociation of Western State Engineers in May 2026 for their Spring Workshop. That presentation focused on the use of GenAI in project management and workflows to share water data for the public using TAPs as an example. During the preparation of that presentation I created a conceptual model that served as a pratical map for moving from internal workflows to public reuse. The purpose of that model was not to represent a completed path, but a work in progress. A key element of building that map is that it served me as vision about how I would like to develop the TAPs packages. During the design of TAPs strategy a key guiding principle was "what can I build today that would set the stage for the future implementation". Particularly, at the early stages I envisioned TAPs not only as a reproducible repository, but also as a knowledge base that could be used in the future to serve as the grounding source for a local AI model that could help future developers understand how the project work and how it was designed and implemented. For that reason the map included stages related to code building and even containerization. The map, or staircase model, became the first portable artifact related to GenAI supported workflows that could be inspected outside the context of TAPs.

Around the same time I connected with a Sr. Developer at Meta who got curious about the use of AI in the context of a natural resources agency. I shared the staircase model with them, and it took several passes to be able to explain the model. Then, they asked if I had a prototype about how the model should work. At that point I only had the TAPs private repository, which could not work as a portable prototype. I asked them how they typically implement GenAI developments across their projects and they expanded on the prototype notion including the concept of dog-fooding, meaning, do you have a product that can be tested by yourself and other users to verify that it produces value. I realized that TAPs could not be that prototype, as its a work that would take a couple of years to be completed. So, I asked myself about ways to generate another portable artifact that could serve as a smaller and packageable version of the workflow. I started by creating smaller packages documenting reasoning about the workflows required to create a presentation, or the application review of a grant cycle. But it was my experience as independent researcher that exposed me to using agentic workflows to create a version of a repository designed precisely to build around reproducibility and reasoning externalization.

## The water-rights snapshot specification and representational choices

- What were you trying to accomplish with the data snapshot specification, and how did your understanding of the parent water-rights database develop during that work?
- What moments made you notice the choices involved in moving from database structures and records toward a specification?
- How did GenAI support, your own interpretation, and encounters with the database or other constraints shape what you understood and specified?
- What surprised you, changed your direction, or remained unresolved?

A core principle of TAPs is that they should be open, reproducible, and rely exclusively on data available through public repositories, like the Oregon Open Data Portal. A critical step for TAPs is to create data snapshot built out of our internal databases that could be versioned and updated on the Open Data Portal, so all the downstream workflows for TAPs products would have that portal as the authoritative source instead of our internal data systems. We have three key datasets that are the core of the first iteration of TAPs: streamgages, well logs, and water rights. Among those three, water rights is the most critical one because OWRD is the owner and maintainer of such data. While external users and planning collaboratives could find information about surface and groundwater in other public databases, that's not the case for water rights data. Yet, the extraction, transformation, loading, and publication of those data through the Open Data Portal require specifications about their individual metadata and how the three datasets can be assembled together into products like maps and other visualizations.

My initial understanding of the Water Rights Information System (WRIS) was that the structure of the database should allow to answer questions about the where, how much, and by whom was water being diverted from our sources (surface and groundwater), so that we could use their spatial structure to represent the cumulative volumes authorized along stream newtorks or small spatial units in a hydrological context, like HUC-12. It turned out that WRIS is not structured to support spatial analyses. It is actually a complex historical records that requires combined interpretation between digital and physical files that span more than a century of history, including the changes in documentation practices driven by the major technological developments that took place during the XX century. So just the assemblage of a query to create a version of the water rights dataset has taken more than a dozen meetings with experts in the database that already retired or are about to retire. So, the learning process through those meetings demonstrated the critical role of human-mediated representational transitions required to build not a complete and accurate, but the most defensible dataset that could support the spatial analyses required by TAPs.

I used GenAI support to create the initial documentation for specifications about the datasets and their integration, with the WR data having a pivotal role as an indicator of legally authorized pressure on both surface and groundwater sources. The representational space in which that hybrid reasoning and design took place was more anchored on the initial (and incomplete) understanding of the database structure, that was only possible after securing time with the experts to dig and improve that representation. Thus, the multiple encounters with constraints and limitations led to an important update about the technical understanding of the WRIS database and the creation of a draft document with GenAI assistance, now based on the lessons learned from those multiple meetings: DRAFT: From Oregon Public Waters to WRIS-Derived Data Products - A conceptual framework for administrative records, analytical models, and dataset governance.

This excerpt of the draft document illustrates one of the biggest realization/surprises along the process:

"WRIS should therefore not be understood as containing one complete digital object for each water right. Its structure reflects the different kinds of records produced through water-right administration. These include administrative records, mapped point-of-diversion records, authorized-use records, place-of-use records, water-use-reporting records, and historical or successive records associated with changes over time.

This structure differs from the common intuition about a water right. A permit, license, or certificate is often imagined as the document that answers all the practical questions about the authorization: who may use the water, where it may be taken from, how much may be diverted, when diversion may occur, what the water may be used for, and where that use is allowed. Because these conditions define how the authorization operates in practice, people naturally expect them to be stored together as the properties of one identifiable water-right record.

Inside WRIS, however, the legal authorization is better understood as the subject being documented rather than as one complete stored record. Its digital representation emerges from multiple connected record families, each describing a different aspect of the authorization or its administrative history."

## A third missing strand - Oregon State AI strategist interest on TAPs and GenAI

In order to have accesss to GenAI support for TAPs, we engaged in a lengthy process with the IT department and the AI Strategist for the State, Nik Blosser, to be able to obtain and Enterprise License Seat for the OHA/ODHS ChatGPT enterprise license. Details about this timeline are included in `nonpublic_notes/timeline.md`. Since it was the first AI authorized project for OWRD requiring AI tools other than Copilot, I had to explain the use case in very detailed way, not only through the ITI (Information Technology Investments) form, but in meetings and informal presentations. This opened an unusual channel of communication with Nik Blosser and also required me to stay up to date, present to leadership, and even provide public comments on the evolving guidance on Responsible AI Usage policies for the state. I was then by OWRD leadership to give a presentation at the AWSE (see above), that resulted in the staircase model, which I then shared with Nik. In our last interaction he asked me to share more specific details about the use of GenAI in TAPs, which prompted me in finding the best communication vehicle to provide those insights, and more importantly, highlight the potential transferability of the ongoing project to other areas of application.

## How these experiences connect to this repository and the talk

- How did these two strands relate in your experience? Did they develop together, separately, or become connected in retrospect?
- Why did creating this repository seem like a useful next step, and what did you hope to learn by using selected workflow practices here?
- Which aspects of the working backbone grew out of these experiences, and which came from other conversations, readings, or prior thinking?
- Where do the experiences fit the backbone, and where do they complicate it or suggest questions it does not yet address?

These two strands were running in parallel within different conversational spaces and work environments, but both were informing the development of TAPs as a product itself and as a strategy related to supporting data-to-decisions workflows. A moment of convergence took place when I realized I had been asking a fundamental question during the conversations about the query design based on my updated understanding of the WRIS database structure. The proponents of the initial querying process had started with a spatial-first, table-second approach to assemble the dataset. That is, they wanted to select a HUC-12 as an area of interest and document all the water rights, points of diversion, and places of use that would fall into that space. I have been asking with lower success if another type of approach could be possible, acknowledging that due to the complexity of the database, a record/table based query should occur first, and then identify the recoverable spatial information of those records. That was a realization that took weeks for the team that I had anticipated, but perhaps poorly articulated throughtout those sessions. I then realized that the reasoning process that led me to my improve understanding of the WRIS database could become an important piece to be included in the WR component of TAPs, not just only the code and interpretation.

At this point, all these parallel excursions started to accumulate outside the main TAPs repository for multiple reasons, including trivial ones like path lenghts for these products were buried in a complex file structure making rendering of the products difficult, and also needing to create products faster while the whole TAPs documentation that include session notes, decision logs, risk logs, session time tracking, ai_session register, were creating an adminstrative governance burden that I found myself avoiding in order to make progress with the project. So, as a use-case, TAPs was becoming harder to communicate without abstracting the core idea behind the workflow, and a simplified point of entry into how such GenAI supported workflow could work.

Here is where the third strand integrates into a specific question, what could be abstracted and improved from the TAPs GenAI support that would inform a productive conversation that would be relevant for leadership at OWRD and the AI strategy office.

While taking stock from the lessons learned from TAPs, the core principle, externalization of reasoning for improved understanding and reproducibility survived. But in this iteration the governance layer focuses more on tracking the consequential moves for the project development in a more proactive way than the original defensive approach of "document everything you can." Also, as the access to agentic capabilities opened up, I realized that part of this administrative governance could be specified through the agents instructions, so, instead of copying and pasting markdown text from the chats into the records, I could integrate the documentation process inside the workspace more seamlessly.
